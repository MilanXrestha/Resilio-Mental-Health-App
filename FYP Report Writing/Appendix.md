## APPENDIX A: DETAILED MENTAL HEALTH STATISTICS IN NEPAL

### A.1 Prevalence of Mental Health Conditions

The following table is derived from the Nepal Mental Health Survey 2020 conducted by the Ministry of Health and Population, which remains the most comprehensive national epidemiological survey of mental health conditions conducted in Nepal.

**Table A.1: Prevalence of Mental Health Conditions in Nepal (MoHP, 2020)**

| Condition | Lifetime Prevalence (%) | 12-Month Prevalence (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Any Mental Disorder | 12.9 | 5.8 |
| Depression | 4.5 | 2.1 |
| Anxiety Disorders | 3.5 | 1.8 |
| Alcohol Use Disorder | 4.0 | 2.5 |
| Suicidal Ideation | 5.9 | 2.3 |
| Suicide Attempts | 1.4 | 0.5 |

Applied to Nepal's estimated population of approximately 30 million, a 12-month prevalence of 5.8% for any mental disorder indicates that approximately 1.74 million Nepalis experience a diagnosable mental health condition in any given year. The 2.1% 12-month prevalence for depression equates to approximately 630,000 individuals. These figures are considered likely underestimates due to stigma-related underreporting and the absence of validated Nepali-language instruments in many rural survey contexts.

The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated the burden. The World Health Organization (2022) reported a 25% global increase in anxiety and depression during the pandemic period. Sigdel et al. (2021) confirmed significantly elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and comorbid conditions in Nepal during the lockdown period, with the disruption of community support systems, economic hardship, and bereavement identified as primary drivers.

### A.2 Treatment Gap

The treatment gap: the proportion of individuals with a diagnosable condition who receive no treatment: is among the most critical indicators of a healthcare system's capacity to serve its population's mental health needs.

**Table A.2: Mental Health Treatment Gap: Nepal vs. International Comparators**

| Indicator | Nepal (%) | Low-Income Country Average (%) | High-Income Country Average (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall Treatment Gap | 92 | 85 | 45 |
| Depression Treatment Gap | 89 | 80 | 50 |
| Severe Mental Disorder Gap | 75 | 70 | 35 |

Source: World Health Organization (2021); Luitel et al. (2015); Patel et al. (2018)

Nepal's 92% overall treatment gap means that nine out of every ten Nepalis with a diagnosable mental health condition receive no treatment whatsoever. This places Nepal among the most underserved countries in the world for mental health care. The economic cost of this treatment gap is significant: the World Health Organization (2016) estimated that every US$1 invested in treatment for depression and anxiety yields a US$4 return through improved health and productivity.

---

### A.3 Mental Health Workforce

The shortage of qualified mental health professionals is the structural constraint that most directly limits Nepal's capacity to reduce the treatment gap. The following table compares Nepal's mental health workforce density with WHO recommended minimum staffing levels.

**Table A.3: Mental Health Workforce Density in Nepal (per 100,000 population)**

| Profession | Nepal (per 100,000) | WHO Recommended Minimum | Shortfall (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychiatrists | 0.36 | 1.0 | 64% shortage |
| Psychologists | 0.08 | 1.0 | 92% shortage |
| Mental Health Nurses | 0.25 | 2.0 | 88% shortage |
| Social Workers | 0.05 | 1.0 | 95% shortage |

Source: WHO (2021); Ministry of Health and Population, Nepal (2020)

At current rates, Nepal has fewer than 150 practising psychiatrists for a population of 30 million. Psychologists are even scarcer, with approximately 24 registered practitioners nationally. The social worker and community mental health nurse workforce is similarly depleted. Nepal's mental health system from a public health perspective has been characterised as operating at a fraction of the capacity required to meet even basic population needs (The Lancet Regional Health Southeast Asia, 2025).

### A.4 Geographic Distribution of Mental Health Services

Nepal's mental health infrastructure is concentrated in the Kathmandu Valley and a small number of secondary urban centres. The following table illustrates the severe geographic inequity in service distribution.

**Table A.4: Geographic Distribution of Population vs. Mental Health Services**

| Region | % of Population | % of Mental Health Services |
|---|---|---|
| Kathmandu Valley | 12% | 65% |
| Other Urban Areas | 18% | 25% |
| Rural Areas | 70% | 10% |

Source: Jordans et al. (2016); Luitel et al. (2015)

This distribution means that 70% of Nepal's population: those living in rural areas: has access to only 10% of the country's mental health services. A patient in a rural hill district requiring psychiatric care faces a journey of multiple hours or days to reach the nearest service provider. For many rural Nepalis, particularly those from lower-income households, this journey is not practically feasible. The consequence is that the treatment gap is not uniformly distributed: it falls most heavily on rural communities, which also tend to face the greatest structural risk factors for mental health conditions, including poverty, conflict legacy, natural disaster exposure, and geographic isolation (Atreya et al., 2023).

### A.5 Barriers to Mental Health Treatment in Nepal

Survey data collected as part of the Nepal Mental Health Survey and corroborated by academic literature identifies the following primary barriers preventing treatment-seeking behaviour among Nepalis with mental health conditions.

**Table A.5: Reported Barriers to Mental Health Treatment (% of respondents identifying barrier)**

| Barrier | % Reporting |
|---|---|
| Cost: treatment is too expensive | 78% |
| Distance: no accessible service nearby | 65% |
| Stigma: fear of social judgement | 62% |
| Not perceived as a medical issue | 48% |
| Lack of awareness of available resources | 45% |
| Preference for traditional healers | 38% |

Source: Kohrt and Harper (2008); Ministry of Health and Population, Nepal (2020); Atreya et al. (2023)

Cost is the dominant barrier, identified by 78% of respondents. The average cost of a private psychiatric consultation in Kathmandu ranges from NPR 1,500 to NPR 3,000 per session, a substantial proportion of the median monthly income of approximately NPR 20,000–25,000 for working adults in urban areas, and entirely out of reach for the rural majority. Stigma remains a critical barrier at 62%: Kohrt and Harper (2008) documented the complex cultural dynamics through which mental health conditions in Nepal are often attributed to spiritual causes, moral failures, or family shame, making help-seeking an act that risks social exclusion. The platform addresses all five primary barriers through its design choices.

## APPENDIX B: EXTENDED PROBLEM ANALYSIS

### B.1 App Fragmentation and Lack of Integration

The mental health application market presents users with a fragmented landscape in which each tool addresses a narrow slice of mental health support. A typical user seeking comprehensive support must install and maintain multiple disconnected applications: a meditation app (e.g., Headspace), a therapy booking platform (e.g., BetterHelp), a mood journaling tool, and a crisis hotline contact. This fragmentation creates significant practical and psychological barriers to sustained engagement.

**Table B.1: Impact of App Fragmentation on User Behaviour**

| Problem | Practical Impact | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple app maintenance | Users discontinue individual apps after weeks | Linardon and Fuller-Tyszkiewicz (2020): 26% drop-off within first week |
| No data continuity | Therapist has no view of mood trends between sessions | Bakker et al. (2016): identified as primary limitation of standalone apps |
| Context switching | Users disengage when transition between tools is required | Fleming et al. (2019): 60% higher retention with integrated tools |
| Inconsistent design | Different UX patterns across apps increase cognitive load | No single source of psychological truth for users |

The consequence is that many users maintain none of the individual tools long enough to derive clinical benefit. Research by Fleming et al. (2019) found that integrated digital mental health interventions: those combining professional access, self-monitoring, and psychoeducational content in a single platform: achieved 60% higher retention compared to standalone app interventions.

---

### B.2 Lack of Professional Integration

A specific and consequential gap in the digital mental health landscape is the absence of platforms that allow clinical professionals to access user-generated data from self-help tools during consultations. The typical BetterHelp or Wysa interaction is entirely siloed from the user's mood logs, questionnaire scores, and game engagement patterns. The therapist arrives at a session with no visibility into the patient's between-session experience.

**Table B.2: Consequences of Professional-App Disconnect**

| Gap | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Therapist has no pre-session patient data | First 10–15 minutes of session spent on retrospective information gathering |
| No continuity of care data | Deteriorating mood trends invisible to clinician between sessions |
| Validated instruments not integrated into care pathway | PHQ-9/GAD-7 scores generated by apps never reach the treating clinician |
| No shared record | Patient must verbally re-narrate emotional history at each session |

Dr. Dibyandra Singh (external client) identified this gap explicitly during the project requirements gathering phase, noting that access to a patient's longitudinal mood log before a session would fundamentally change the quality of therapeutic interaction.

---

### B.3 Long-Term Engagement Failures

The mental health app literature consistently identifies engagement and retention as the critical failure mode of digital mental health interventions. Initial adoption is often high: the novelty effect drives downloads: but sustained use over the timescales clinically necessary to produce benefit is rare.

**Table B.3: Engagement and Retention Challenges in Mental Health Apps**

| Challenge | Observed Impact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Lack of accountability | No external motivation to return | Bakker et al. (2016) |
| Absence of professional relationship | Users disengage when the app cannot respond adaptively | Fleming et al. (2019) |
| Overemphasis on daily streaks | Streak-break leads to complete abandonment | Chandrashekar (2018) |
| Content staleness | Users exhaust available content within weeks | Industry observation |
| No tangible outcome feedback | Users cannot see the impact of consistent usage | Shiffman et al. (2008) |

Resilio's engagement design: achievement-based rather than streak-based, with continuously updated professional content and therapist-visible mood data: is specifically architected to address these documented failure modes.

---

### B.4 Connectivity and Infrastructure Requirements

International mental health platforms are designed for high-bandwidth, always-on connectivity environments. This design assumption renders them functionally unusable in significant portions of Nepal where 3G or 4G coverage is intermittent, absent, or unaffordably expensive.

**Table B.4: Connectivity Challenges in the Nepal Context**

| Challenge | Impact on International Platforms |
|---|---|
| Rural 3G-only coverage | Video consultation platforms require stable 4G; sessions drop or never initiate |
| Data cost sensitivity | Unlimited data plans unavailable in rural areas; users budget MB carefully |
| Intermittent connectivity | Session state not preserved on disconnection in most platforms |
| Offline incapability | Mood logging and content access impossible without active connection |

Source: Nepal Telecommunications Authority (2022); DataReportal (2025)

Approximately 30% of Nepal's population lacks regular internet access (Nepal Telecommunications Authority, 2022). Even among urban users with 4G access, data cost sensitivity makes the unconstrained video streaming model of BetterHelp economically inaccessible for extended use. Resilio's SQLite-backed offline architecture, LRU content caching, and adaptive-quality video transmission address these constraints directly.

### B.5 User Journey Without an Integrated Solution

The following illustrates the typical journey of a Nepali adult with a mental health need attempting to access support through the fragmented current landscape, prior to the availability of an integrated solution such as Resilio.

**Table B.5: Pre-Resilio User Journey: 7-Stage Problem Experience**

| Stage | User Experience | Barrier Encountered |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Recognition | User becomes aware of persistent low mood or anxiety | Stigma; lack of awareness that the experience is treatable |
| 2. Information seeking | User searches online; finds general content in English | Content not localised; no clear pathway to professional help |
| 3. App download | User downloads international app (Headspace, BetterHelp) | Credit card required for payment; high cost in USD; English only |
| 4. Abandonment | User cannot pay or finds content irrelevant | Cost barrier; payment method not available |
| 5. In-person search | User seeks local psychiatrist or counsellor | Distance; waiting lists; cost; stigma of physical attendance |
| 6. No care obtained | User discontinues help-seeking | All pathways blocked |
| 7. Worsening condition | Untreated condition deteriorates | No follow-up mechanism; no crisis detection |

This journey characterises the experience of the majority of Nepalis with untreated mental health needs. Resilio's design intent is to interrupt this journey at stages 3 and 4 by eliminating the cost and payment barriers, and to provide an integrated pathway that prevents the abandonment characteristic of stages 4 through 7.

### D.7 User Journey with Resilio (4 Phases)

**Phase 1: Discovery and Registration**
User downloads Resilio; registers via OTP or Google Sign-In; completes preference onboarding (preferred language, areas of concern, therapy goals); receives matched therapist recommendations.

**Phase 2: Exploration and Self-Assessment**
User accesses content hub; logs first mood entry; completes PHQ-9 and GAD-7; engages with wellness games; receives first achievement badge.

**Phase 3: Professional Engagement**
User browses therapist directory; selects matched therapist; books appointment via eSewa; receives confirmation and reminder notifications; attends live video session; therapist views patient mood data in consultation panel.

**Phase 4: Sustained Engagement**
User continues daily mood logging; returns for follow-up sessions; progresses through achievement tiers; accesses updated content from therapist content hub; mood trend visible to both user and therapist over time.

## APPENDIX E: METHODOLOGY DETAILS

### E.1 Methodology Options Evaluated

Four software development methodologies were evaluated for suitability to this project: Agile (Scrum), Waterfall, Rapid Application Development (Prototype), and the Rational Unified Process (RUP).

**Table E.1: Agile (Scrum): Strengths and Weaknesses**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Iterative delivery allows early feedback | Requires continuous stakeholder availability |
| Adapts readily to changing requirements | Sprint planning overhead for a solo developer |
| Well-suited to evolving UX requirements | Formal ceremonies (standups, retrospectives) less applicable solo |
| Strong community tooling support | Backlog management can expand scope uncontrollably |
| Encourages continuous integration practices | Less documentation emphasis may create academic report gaps |

Source: Beck et al. (2001)

---

**Table E.2: Waterfall: Strengths and Weaknesses**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Clear phase deliverables and documentation | No flexibility once requirements are baselined |
| Well-understood by academic evaluators | Any requirements change requires full phase restart |
| Straightforward progress tracking | Late discovery of requirement errors is very costly |
| Suitable for well-defined, stable requirements | Unsuitable for projects with evolving UX or stakeholder input |
| Comprehensive documentation at each phase | Testing occurs only at end; defects discovered late |

Source: Sommerville (2016); Pressman (2014)

---

**Table E.3: Rapid Application Development (Prototype): Strengths and Weaknesses**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Fast initial prototype for user feedback | Prototypes may become permanent, accumulating technical debt |
| High user involvement | Scope creep risk when users see early working versions |
| Suitable for UX-driven requirements | Poor documentation discipline common |
| Identifies usability issues early | Architecture quality can be sacrificed for speed |
| Flexible and iterative | Less suitable for complex backend or security-sensitive systems |

Source: Martin (1991)

---

**Table E.4: Rational Unified Process (RUP): Strengths and Weaknesses**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Iterative within defined phases (Inception/Elaboration/Construction/Transition) | More documentation overhead than Agile |
| Balances flexibility and structure | Can be heavyweight for small teams |
| Use-case driven architecture aligns with role-based system design | RUP training and tooling less common than Agile |
| Risk mitigation prioritised in Elaboration phase | Requires discipline to avoid waterfall-like phase locking |
| Academic report structure maps naturally to RUP phases | Phase boundaries require explicit management |
| Supports concurrent workflows within each phase | Less intuitive for developers without formal methodology training |

Source: Kruchten (2003)

---

### E.2 Methodology Comparison Matrix

The following matrix evaluates each methodology against eight criteria specific to this project's constraints and requirements.

**Table E.5: Methodology Comparison Matrix**

| Criterion | Agile (Scrum) | Waterfall | Prototype (RAD) | RUP | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flexibility to changing requirements | High | Low | High | Medium-High | High |
| Documentation quality for FYP report | Medium | High | Low | High | High |
| User/stakeholder involvement | High | Low | Very High | High | Medium |
| Risk management structure | Medium | Low | Low | High | High |
| Iterative development support | High | None | High | High | High |
| Deadline alignment (academic calendar) | Medium | High | Medium | High | High |
| Suitability for solo developer | Medium | High | High | High | Medium |
| Academic framework recognition | High | High | Medium | High | Medium |

**Rationale for RUP Selection**

RUP achieved the strongest overall profile across all eight criteria relevant to this project. Its four-phase structure (Inception, Elaboration, Construction, Transition) maps directly onto the academic project lifecycle: requirements gathering and feasibility, architectural prototyping and design, iterative feature development, and testing and deployment: providing a clear framework for both development management and FYP report chapter organisation.

RUP's emphasis on use-case-driven architecture was directly applicable to a three-role system (Customer, Therapist, Administrator) where the primary design artefacts are role-specific use case diagrams. The Elaboration phase's focus on risk mitigation through architectural prototyping was valuable in identifying WebRTC integration and SuperTokens authentication as high-risk components requiring early validation. Its documentation discipline ensures that all required FYP deliverables: use cases, sequence diagrams, SRS, test cases: are produced as natural artefacts of the process rather than retrofitted after development.

### I.1 Flutter: SuperTokens OTP Authentication Flow

The following code is taken from `lib/features/customer/auth/data/datasources/remote/supertokens_data_source.dart` and demonstrates the three-step SuperTokens passwordless OTP authentication flow used to authenticate customers.

```dart
// Step 1: Request OTP be sent to the user's email address
Future<OtpSessionData> createCode({required String email}) async {
  final response = await _dio.post(
    ApiEndpoints.stCreateCode,
    data: {'email': email},
    options: Options(
      responseType: ResponseType.json,
      headers: {'Content-Type': 'application/json', 'rid': 'passwordless'},
    ),
  );
  final body = response.data as Map<String, dynamic>;
  if (body['status'] != 'OK') throw Exception('SuperTokens createCode error');
  return OtpSessionData(
    preAuthSessionId: body['preAuthSessionId'] as String,
    deviceId: body['deviceId'] as String,
  );
}

// Step 2: Verify the OTP entered by the user
Future<Map<String, dynamic>> consumeCode({
  required String preAuthSessionId,
  required String deviceId,
  required String userInputCode,
}) async {
  final response = await _dio.post(
    ApiEndpoints.stConsumeCode,
    data: {
      'preAuthSessionId': preAuthSessionId,
      'deviceId': deviceId,
      'userInputCode': userInputCode,
    },
    options: Options(
      responseType: ResponseType.json,
      headers: {'Content-Type': 'application/json', 'rid': 'passwordless'},
    ),
  );
  final body = response.data as Map<String, dynamic>;
  final accessTokenFromHeader = response.headers.value('st-access-token');
  if (accessTokenFromHeader != null) body['accessToken'] = accessTokenFromHeader;
  return body;
}

// Step 3: Sync authenticated user into Supabase backend
Future<Map<String, dynamic>> completePasswordlessLogin({
  required String email,
  required String accessToken,
}) async {
  final response = await _dio.post(
    ApiEndpoints.passwordlessComplete,
    data: {'email': email},
    options: Options(
      responseType: ResponseType.json,
      headers: {
        'Content-Type': 'application/json',
        'Authorization': 'Bearer $accessToken',
      },
    ),
  );
  return response.data as Map<String, dynamic>;
}
```

```dart
Future<void> _bookSlot() async {
  if (_selectedSlot == null || _bookingInProgress) return;
  setState(() => _bookingInProgress = true);
  try {
    final slot = _slotsForDate[_selectedSlot!];
    final roomId = 'room_${widget.therapistId}_${slot.millisecondsSinceEpoch}';
    final response = await _dio.post('/appointments', data: {
      'therapistId': widget.therapistId,
      'scheduledTime': slot.toUtc().toIso8601String(),
      'meetingRoomId': roomId,
    });
    final appointmentId = response.data['id'];
    final fee = (_therapist?['consultation_fee'] ?? 500.0).toDouble();

    EsewaFlutterSdk.initPayment(
      eSewaConfig: ESewaConfig(
        env: Environment.test,
        clientId: _esewaClientId,
        secretId: _esewaSecretId,
      ),
      eSewaPayment: ESewaPayment(
        productId: appointmentId,
        productName: 'Therapy Session',
        productPrice: fee.toString(),
        callbackUrl: '',
      ),
      onPaymentSuccess: (result) => _createAppointmentAfterPayment(result),
      onPaymentFailure: (_) => _onPaymentFailure(),
      onPaymentCancellation: (_) => _onPaymentCancelled(),
    );
  } catch (e) {
    setState(() {
      _error = 'Booking failed. Please try again.';
      _bookingInProgress = false;
    });
  }
}
```

### I.3 Backend: Appointment Repository and Payment Verification

The following code is taken from the Node.js backend and demonstrates the appointment data layer and payment verification controller.

```javascript
class AppointmentRepository {
  async createAppointment(data) {
    const { data: appointment, error } = await supabase
      .from('appointments')
      .insert({
        patient_id: data.patientId,
        therapist_id: data.therapistId,
        scheduled_time: data.scheduledTime,
        status: 'pending',
        payment_status: 'pending',
        meeting_room_id: data.meetingRoomId,
      })
      .select()
      .single();
    if (error) throw error;
    return this._map(appointment);
  }

  async updateStatus(id, status, paymentStatus = null, transactionId = null) {
    const payload = { status };
    if (paymentStatus) payload.payment_status = paymentStatus;
    if (transactionId) payload.payment_transaction_id = transactionId;
    const { data, error } = await supabase
      .from('appointments')
      .update(payload)
      .eq('id', id)
      .select()
      .single();
    if (error) throw error;
    return this._map(data);
  }
}

// Payment verification endpoint
router.post('/payment/verify', authMiddleware, async (req, res) => {
  const { appointmentId, transactionId, totalAmount } = req.body;
  try {
    const appointment = await appointmentRepo.getAppointment(appointmentId);
    if (!appointment) return res.status(404).json({ error: 'Not found' });
    const updated = await appointmentRepo.updateStatus(
      appointmentId, 'confirmed', 'paid', transactionId
    );
    const therapist = await userRepo.getUser(appointment.therapistId);
    if (therapist?.fcmToken) {
      await fcmService.notifyNewBooking(therapist.fcmToken, updated);
    }
    return res.json({ success: true, appointment: updated });
  } catch (err) {
    return res.status(500).json({ error: err.message });
  }
});
```

## APPENDIX M: SOFTWARE REQUIREMENTS SPECIFICATION (SRS)

### M.1 Introduction

**M.1.1 Purpose**

This Software Requirements Specification defines the complete functional and non-functional requirements for Resilio, a mobile mental health platform for Nepal. It is intended as a reference document for development, testing, and stakeholder validation.

**M.1.2 Scope**

Resilio is a cross-platform mobile application supporting three user roles: customers (patients and wellness users), therapists, and administrators. It encompasses therapist discovery, appointment booking and eSewa payment, live WebRTC video and audio consultation, wellness content hub, wellness games, mood tracking, clinical questionnaires, and role-specific dashboards.

**M.1.3 Definitions**

- **Customer:** A registered user seeking mental health support
- **Therapist:** A verified licensed mental health professional using the platform
- **Administrator:** A platform operator with full management access
- **SuperTokens:** The authentication framework used for OTP, Google, and Facebook login
- **WebRTC:** The real-time communication standard used for video and audio consultations
- **eSewa:** Nepal's primary digital wallet payment gateway
- **SQLite:** The local on-device database used for offline data persistence

### M.2 Overall Description

**M.2.1 Product Perspective**

Resilio operates as a three-tier system: Flutter mobile frontend, Node.js/Express backend API, and PostgreSQL database via Supabase. External services include SuperTokens (authentication), Firebase (notifications and Firestore), Cloudinary (media), eSewa (payments), and WebRTC/Socket.IO (consultations).

**M.2.2 User Classes**

| User Class | Access Level | Primary Interface |
|---|---|---|
| Customer | Standard | Customer dashboard, games, content, booking |
| Therapist | Elevated | Therapist dashboard, session management, content publishing |
| Administrator | Full | Admin dashboard, platform management |

**M.2.3 Operating Environment**

- Mobile OS: Android 8.0 (API 26) and above; iOS 13 and above (future)
- Connectivity: Functional on 4G, 3G, and offline (core features)
- Language: English (Nepali localisation in progress)

| Crisis response | PHQ-9 severe score (≥20) triggers Nepal Mental Health Helpline notice |

| Account deletion | Users can request full data deletion from within the application |
